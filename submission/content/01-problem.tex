\section{The decision, and what is missing from it}

An issuer authorising a card transaction has three actions, not two: approve, decline, or
challenge the cardholder through 3-D Secure. The middle one is what makes the problem tractable
at scale --- routing even one percent of authorisation volume to human analysts is not
operationally possible, while a step-up challenge is. Fraud models are reported by AUC or
average precision; neither is a statement about a decision, and neither carries a finite-sample
guarantee. Conformal methods reach this problem closely --- \Cited{Singh et al.} pair
class-conditional prediction with cost-controlled abstention, \Cited{Zhu et al.} certify a
false-negative rate on card fraud, \Cited{Nayak and Bushara} report a fraud objective their
own paper calls conformal-style rather than a finite-sample guarantee, and
\Cited{Chen et al.} carry the temporal case to a graph model --- but none conditions the
guarantee on the abstention region it routes to, and those that do order time certify a
prediction set rather than a decision. Selection-conditional risk control does condition on a
retained region \Cited{(Xu et al.; Bai and Jin)}, under exchangeability, not on payments, and on
the opposite half: it defers rejected cases uncertified to \Cited{``human review''} --- the
band certified here --- as does the concurrent \Cited{Deng et al.}
Both are what this submission adds, and the second turns out to matter.

\textbf{What we contribute.} A three-valued rule whose \emph{abstention band} carries a
distribution-free, finite-sample risk certificate under a pre-registration frozen before any
model was fitted, and an honest measurement of where two quantum approaches sit against a strong
classical baseline on the same decision.

\textbf{What a successful PoC would demonstrate.} A step-up decision whose false-decline rate
carries a distribution-free, finite-sample certificate at a level the bank sets, validated on a
block it never saw --- the artefact a model-risk function can audit, which an AUC cannot
provide. This study meets that target on public data at loose levels only
($\alpha \ge 0.10$) and at the band budgets the sample size permits; the PoC target is the same
property at the budget the business chooses, not the one the data forces.

\textbf{What the rule does to a day of traffic.} On the held-out block, at the certified
configuration with the tightest $\alpha$: \ClaimOperatingApproveShare\,\% approved outright,
\ClaimOperatingStepUpShare\,\% routed to a 3-D~Secure challenge, \ClaimOperatingDeclineShare\,\%
declined outright, and \ClaimOperatingTerminalDeclineShare\,\% declined in total once the band's
own declines are counted. The middle number is the whole argument for a three-valued rule, and it is the
number a bank can price before it believes anything else in this document.

\textbf{Expected impact.} The deliverable is an operating guarantee, not a score, and three
consequences follow, none needing a quantum result. A model-risk function
can review a bound instead of a metric, which is what validation and monitoring
\Cited{SR 26-2} asks of a decisioning model and an AUC cannot supply --- and it matters more
here, not less, because fraud detection reads on the \Cited{EU AI Act} Annex~III
\emph{exception}, so no external high-risk regime supplies that discipline. The operating point becomes an input: the business
chooses $\alpha$ and the band budget, and the certificate reports whether that choice is
attainable. And the band bounds what the expensive component costs ---
Section~\ref{sec:feasibility} prices it against the authorisation budget --- which is what makes
a quantum re-ranker discussable at all. The statement prices the other side itself ---
\Cited{\$443bn in 2021} falsely declined globally, \Cited{40\,\%+} of customers abandoning after
one --- and a certified bound on the rate is what turns that into a number a bank can act on. The
issuer-side cost is not public, so we certify the \emph{rate} and leave the bank to multiply
it.

\textbf{Neither quantum arm produced a usable result, and they failed differently.} A fidelity
quantum kernel was rejected by its own a-priori screens before it was run on the task --- a
decisive negative. A matrix-product-state classifier ran and did not beat gradient boosting, but
that comparison is underpowered, so what it yields is a non-superiority bound rather than a null
result.

\textbf{What we do not claim.} No quantum advantage of any kind. No portfolio-level PSD2
compliance figure --- the SCA-RTS ceilings govern exemption eligibility, not a decline threshold,
and the appendix records that category error as one we made and withdrew. And no claim that the
certificate binds the \emph{unconditional} false-decline rate: it does not, and
Section~\ref{sec:method} says why that is the point rather than a caveat.
