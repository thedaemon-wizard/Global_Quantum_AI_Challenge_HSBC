\section{Two quantum arms, both negative}

\paragraph{The kernel never reached the task.} Before running a fidelity quantum kernel we
pre-registered screens over \ClaimScreenConfigs{} configurations and gated on two of them (encoding $\times$ qubits $\times$ bandwidth
$\times$ entanglement). A kernel is usable only if it is not exponentially concentrated ---
effective rank $r_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i \sigma_i)^2 / (n \sum_i \sigma_i^2)$ inside a
usable band --- \emph{and} not reproducible by a tuned RBF, $\rho_{\mathrm{RBF}} < 0.60$.

\ClaimScreenPassConditioning{} of \ClaimScreenConfigs{} pass conditioning. \textbf{\ClaimScreenPassBoth{} pass
both.} The closest any configuration came to distinctness was
$\rho_{\mathrm{RBF}} = \ClaimScreenMinRbfCorrelation$. Every kernel we could construct on
this data is, to within measurement, a classical RBF in disguise: the median correlation is
\ClaimScreenMedianRbf{} and the maximum is \ClaimScreenMaxRbf{}, indistinguishable to
floating point. We would not have known had we reported a downstream accuracy instead.

\textbf{One pre-registered screen was not gated, and it is the one that would have helped.}
The protocol names the Huang geometric difference first; the implementation gates on the
conditioning and distinctness screens and computes the geometric difference without
consulting it. Huang treats a \emph{large} difference as favourable, so gating on it would
have made this rejection less decisive rather than more. It was found in an audit rather than
decided, and amendment~A6 records it. The screens are also computed on
\ClaimScreenSamples{} stratified rows with at most eight features, which bounds how far
"every kernel we could construct" reaches.

The closest published comparator agrees. A controlled benchmark of quantum kernel methods on
real card-transaction data \Cited{(Faryad, arXiv:2608.15718, August 2026)} reports no robust
quantum advantage, with \Cited{all effect sizes below 0.013 ARI}, the sign of the difference dependent on
register size, and additional qubits degrading performance through kernel concentration. Its
methodological finding is the more useful one for us: a search-budget ablation turned that
paper's one significant advantage into a procedural artefact. Matching search budgets and
fixing the screens in advance is what stops the same thing happening here.

\paragraph{The tensor network ran, and did not win.} A matrix-product-state classifier
contracts $f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\{s\}} A^{s_1} \cdots A^{s_N} \prod_j \phi^{s_j}(x_j)$ with
$\phi(x) = [\cos\frac{\pi x}{2}, \sin\frac{\pi x}{2}]$, sweeping the bond dimension $\chi$
rather than tuning it. In the band, on identical rows and features, it reaches average
precision \ClaimMpsApBestChi{} at the largest bond dimension against \ClaimGbdtApBand{} for
the tuned gradient-boosted baseline, a paired difference of \ClaimMpsDeltaApBestChi.

\paragraph{At full scale it loses by a wider margin, and the margin is all the arm supports.}
On all 431 features, trained on $\Dtrain$ and scored once on $\Dtest$, the tensor network
lands between \ClaimMpsFullAucSeedMin{} and \ClaimMpsFullAucSeedMax{} ROC AUC and between
\ClaimMpsFullApSeedMin{} and \ClaimMpsFullApSeedMax{} average precision across four seeds at
a fixed bond dimension, against \ClaimGbdtFullAucMin--\ClaimGbdtFullAucMax{} and
\ClaimGbdtFullApMin--\ClaimGbdtFullApMax{} for the tuned baseline. Every draw loses by a
factor of two to three on average precision, which is the metric that matters at a
\ClaimTestFraudRate\,\% positive rate.

\textbf{We do not report capacity dependence at full scale, because we cannot resolve it.}
The spread across those four seeds at one bond dimension is \ClaimMpsFullAucSeedSpread{} ROC
AUC and \ClaimMpsFullApSeedSpread{} average precision. The spread the sweep shows \emph{across} bond dimension is
\ClaimMpsFullAucChiSpread{} and \ClaimMpsFullApChiSpread{} --- the same size. A per-configuration reading of that sweep would be reading seed noise, and an
earlier draft of this paragraph did exactly that by quoting the best of four draws.

The seed sweep is affordable only because a matrix product state has no qubit ceiling, and
because its cost turns out not to depend on the bond dimension the way the ansatz suggests:
the four jobs took \ClaimMpsFullSecondsMin{} to \ClaimMpsFullSecondsMax{} seconds against
the sixty-four-fold spread a $\chi^2$ model predicts. A 431-site chain is bound by the launch
overhead of its sequential contractions, not by their arithmetic, so capacity is nearly free
and depth is the cost.

\paragraph{The gate meant to tell us whether that was informative was itself wrong.} The
pre-registration requires the minimum detectable effect to be computed \emph{before} the
comparison, and commits to declaring the test underpowered if it exceeds \ClaimPowerCeiling{}
average precision. Two defects followed. First, the calculation had no caller at all, so the
comparison would have been \emph{observed} rather than tested; the script now refuses to
start without it. Second, our noise estimate was the seed-to-seed spread of the baseline
against itself, standard error \ClaimPowerSeWithinFamily. But this comparison is between
model \emph{families}, whose measured standard error is \ClaimMpsStandardErrorBestChi{} ---
\ClaimPowerSeCrossFamily{} once measured against a second classical family --- roughly
eightfold larger --- so the minimum detectable effect this comparison actually supports is
\ClaimPowerMde{}, which \emph{exceeds} the pre-registered ceiling of \ClaimPowerCeiling.

\textbf{H4 is therefore reported as underpowered}, which is what the protocol commits to when
this happens. What survives is weaker than a null result and we state it as such: every
interval contains zero, so any improvement from the tensor network is bounded above by
$+\ClaimMpsCiHighBestChi{}$ average precision. That is a non-superiority bound. It is not evidence of
equivalence, and it is not evidence that the tensor network is worse.
